Customer-Centric Load Reduction Programs

By Charlie Hewitt · 13.07.2015

As the summer heat sets in, residential air conditioning load delivers its annual punch to the power grid. Engaging customers in active energy management has long been an elusive goal of most utilities. Traditional utility messaging on conservation only goes so far in reducing peak load. Some consumers take these messages to heart, while others disregard them. 

However, the growth of the residential solar energy market, web-enabled thermostats, and smart meters are increasing consumer energy awareness. With energy management moving into the mainstream of consumer consciousness, opportunities for meaningful engagement are emerging. The challenge is to make these programs customer-centric rather than utility-centric.

Old School Programs

Direct load control (DLC) programs are an example of current attempts to manage peak residential demand. DLC programs focus on limiting air conditioner cycling during critical events.

Curtailment is limited to power grid emergencies or times when wholesale market prices spike. The number and duration of load reduction events is contractually limited. In exchange for providing the utility this option, the customer receives a bill credit. Annual incentives range from $20 with the Indianapolis Power & Light CoolCents program up to $200 with the Southern California Edison Summer Discount Plan.

DLC programs have been popular with customers. A 2010 FERC survey identified more than 300 residential DLC programs with over 5 million participants. Utility customer participation rates are typically range from 10-30%. 

Similar to how compact fluorescent lighting is viewed as a transitional technology, DLC may be an intermediate stop on the road to a more energy efficient society. First, many consumers enroll in these programs on the belief that the reward, regardless of how small, offsets the possible curtailment of their air conditioning use. The limited number of historical curtailments supports this view. 

Second, DLC programs are utility-centric. The focus is solely on utility peak load management during extreme circumstances. Customer comfort, ongoing engagement, and sustained conservation are not primary considerations. 

The new wave of load control programs are focused more on technology and customer participation than the simplistic DLC programs of the last decade. This marks a more collaborative and participative approach to energy conservation. The two main program categories are demand response and behavioral demand response.

Demand Response Programs

Demand response programs are similar to DLC programs in that consumers receive financial incentives for reducing electricity usage during times when the power grid is strained. Unlike DLC programs, demand response is a voluntary program and gives consumers the opportunity to reduce load from all end uses rather just air conditioning. 

For example, one electricity provider limits load reduction events to 1pm and 7pm on summer days and from 6pm to 8pm on winter days.  Customers are notified of the event by email and text message. If the customer reduces their usage by the amount specified in the notice, they receive a 5% discount on the following month’s bill.

Demand response is customer-centric in that the consumer elects how, or if, they want to achieve the load reduction. The customer can combine heating and cooling adjustments with reductions in lighting and discretionary uses like laundry and dishwashing. This makes it possible for the consumer to achieve the target reduction in a manner that best suits their needs.

Behavioral Demand Response Programs

Behavioral demand response programs leverage smart thermostat technology to reduce load with minimal discomfort to the consumer. Nest Labs has collaborated with utilities to offer a load reduction program integrated with its learning thermostat.

In the day-ahead concept of this program, the customer is notified of the curtailment event. Nest will pre-cool the home before the event and then allow the temperature to rise a few degrees above the set point during the event. Following the event, the thermostat resumes normal operation.

The attraction of this program is that the customer never loses control of the thermostat. They can adjust the thermostat and essentially opt-out of the curtailment at any time.  Naturally, customers receive a greater financial incentive if they do not override the thermostat during the events.

CPS Energy, for example, offers an $85 enrollment reward plus an additional $30 annual reward if the consumer participates in all curtailment events. There is a limit of 16 three-hour events each summer on weekdays between 1pm and 7 pm. Events are further limited to one per day and no more than three days in a row.

A 2013 program study by Nest revealed a residential load reduction of 1.18 kW per device during each curtailment event. Furthermore, only 14.5% of program participants opted out by changing the thermostat setting during the event.

The potential for much broader participation in this type of program means lower load reductions in each home are needed to achieve the desired result. Necessary temperature adjustments of one degree or less could further reduce the override rates observed in the 2013 study. Additionally, there are seasonal and annual energy savings advantages to web-enabled thermostats beyond critical load events. DLC programs will most likely phase out as these customer-centric approaches to load management become more widely available.
