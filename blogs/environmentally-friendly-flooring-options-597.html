Environmentally Friendly Flooring Options

By Sarah Smith · 02.09.2014

If you are investigating options for renovating an older home or building a new domicile, it’s likely that you may want to combine both economic value and eco-friendly materials that will withstand years of wear and tear. Below, we’ll examine a few of the better options that satisfy these criteria. This article should serve as a good starting place. It will offer solutions around which you can craft a beautiful, safe, and ecologically responsible living space—from the ground up.



Salvaged Hardwood


This option has become increasingly popular in recent decades for a variety of reasons. First, many individuals find the aged look of reclaimed timber particularly appealing. Taken from older homes and business forums, such as warehouses or counting houses, the wood is resurfaced and cut to meet existing demands. Salvaged planks offer a look of established maturity to any room, which many decorators find to be the perfect palette for design concepts incorporating rustic matte finishes and somber color schemes.


Another reason for its desirability is its durability. Unlike many wood surfaces produced today, older timber was often taken from denser growth trees, with tighter and more established internal structure—a denser grain—so that even traditional “soft” woods, such as pine, cedar, and cyprus offer increased resilience over current products. It incorporates a “reuse” ethos into your entire design, which is not only beneficial to the environment, but also offers you a better quality floor.



Vinyl Tiles


While many often assume that vinyl is automatically “bad,” when it comes to flooring, it’s one of the better options for high-traffic areas, homes or schools with small children, and places like hospitals, animal clinics, or workshops. There are several reasons for this. First, it’s not chemically reactive—which means spills of any sort, except the most corrosive of bases and acids won’t make a dent. Second, it actually becomes stronger over time. The older vinyl is, the denser its matrix is, and the more resilient it becomes. It retains color and pattern far better than other flooring options such as linoleum. It won’t scar, weather, or degrade in the same way that substances like linoleum do.



Decorative Concrete


Not only is this one of the most versatile and trendy materials to use in architecture these days, concrete is also economic and eco-friendly. Through the use of a concrete polymer, such as urethane or more accurately polyurethane, these floors last for decades. And because the concrete polymer is often applied as thin layers of color and texture atop a basic substrate, new coatings may be applied to change the look whenever desired. The unique nature of urethane, when applied as a material coating, is that it may be slightly plastic or malleable, or offer a hard, unyielding finish. This renders it ideal for use within and around homes, for floors of all varieties.


There are a number of wonderful applications for these materials and others, such as bamboo, which is a resilient species of giant grass. However, what every homeowner or entrepreneur should consider is the peripheral costs associated with their flooring material of choice. One example is that bamboo is often shipped internationally. The costs of fuel and transport actually make it less economic and ecologically viable as a flooring solution than stamped concrete, which incorporates known organic chemical compounds. Smart designs will take all of these many factors into account when assessing the benefits of a given material.
