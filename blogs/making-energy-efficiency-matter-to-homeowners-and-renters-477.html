Making Energy Efficiency Matter to Homeowners and Renters

By Kelsey Swindler · 24.09.2013

For too long the argument for energy efficiency has been backed by complicated benefit calculations and a lackluster marketing effort. We already know that green packaging does not significantly attract buyers on either side of the political divide, that humans (by nature) struggle with the idea of longterm paybacks (and, understandably, do not find them overly motivating), and that the "save the planet, go green" mentality does not play into the evolutionary fabric of human DNA (whereas a "protect the planet to save your direct genetic offspring" call to action would be much more effective).

Add to this that utility energy efficiency programs focused on community engagement can't even determine a single good way to calculate energy efficiency cost vs. benefits analysis, and you can begin to understand the uphill slog for utilities trying to take on ambitious energy efficiency programs.

Matt Golden's article for Greentech Media explores this problem, describing the various models that have been used to quantify the benefits of energy efficiency measures. As he explains, though, these are just not effective for homeowners. They've become overly complicated (attempting to quantify comfort, satisfactions, durability, and a number of other abstract, and personal, factors) and illogical (factoring in costs that the consumer never even sees).

So how do we make the cost vs. benefits of energy efficiency accurate, comprehensive, and motivating? Golden suggests we return to a simple equation:

"It is time that we take the M&V magic out of energy efficiency and keep it simple (stupid). The public is investing in energy efficiency so we can build fewer power plants, so let's keep it to that. We need to stop trying to decide for consumers what is in their best interest, and stop telling the market and industry that we should only be paying attention to a single benefit...We need to start measuring the results of our work in a way that is transparent and that creates real accountability, and we need to simply measure the necessary public investment to harvest the resource. It is time to start comparing apples to apples -- public or ratepayer dollars against savings at the meter."

The benefit to this simple solution is that it returns to the goal of energy efficiency programs: to create systems that can reduce demand for energy (thus lessening the load on power plants, making energy dollars more cost-effective).

The downside is that it, in turn, may not be very motivating to consumers. We would like to think that we care about how many power plants are built, but the reality is that unless they are built in our backyard, most of us won't care enough to change our daily habits. What is motivating is the idea of a comfortable, well-sealed home, added value to windows and insulation, improved indoor air quality, and financial freedom.

So is it the role of the customer engagement program facilitator and that entity alone to communicate these "added" values? Or is it the role of the utility to factor in these added values into the "benefits" of energy efficiency programs?

And beyond even these questions, how does the home performance contractor add to--and communicate--this value? 

This is where effective community engagement programs will have to step forward, communicating often abstract and intangible value to homeowners and renters and advocating for lower cost means of achieving similar outcomes. There are a number of free behavioral changes that appeal to families of all income levels and have a real impact on home energy costs. 

Until the behavioral, social, and cultural implications of energy efficiency are properly weighed, the true benefit of energy savings--for the individual, the family, and even the community--cannot be truly understood.
