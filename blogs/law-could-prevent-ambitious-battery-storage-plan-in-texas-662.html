Law Could Prevent Ambitious Battery Storage Plan in Texas

By Holbert Janson · 17.02.2015

The transmission utility responsible for the electricity infrastructure of much of Texas has an idea that could change the power industry. Oncor, who is responsible for transmitting power to large parts of the state including the Dallas/Fort Worth area wants to invest billions of dollars in large scale battery storage to help manage the uneven demand for electricity and improve the reliability of the region's electric grid.  Batteries would allow the utility to store power during times of low demand and feed the power back to the grid during times when the state’s power plants are struggling to keep up with demand or when power failures would otherwise leave customers without electricity. This large scale battery storage would seem to be the perfect complement to Texas’ large portfolio of wind energy. However, Oncor's proposal faces several hurdles, including current Texas state law, and opposition from unlikely sources.

Texas deregulation broke up the old monopoly utilities into separate companies that perform different roles in the market. There are electricity producers who own power plants, retail electricity providers who sell electricity to end users, and transmission companies that are responsible for the transmission lines and moving power across the state. As a transmission provider, Oncor is forbidden from selling electricity on the wholesale market—only power generators are allowed to do that. While it’s widely agreed that batteries are a potentially effective way for Texas power utilities to continue to meet the growing demands of its population, generators are not in a position to invest in batteries because they can't really monetize the expense. Power storage has the advantages of availability and reliability, but those are benefits that help a transmission and distribution provider like Oncor, not factors that would increase market prices, so power generators have no way to fund that expense.

And yet running a network of such batteries is tantamount to generating power; the proposed 5,000 megawatts (5GW) in capacity of the batteries that Oncor wants to install throughout its service area is equivalent to about four nuclear power plants added to the grid. With such efficiency and added capacity, some older, inefficient power plants, such as the creaky old coal-fired plants that are even now only used when demand is exceedingly high, might be scuttled entirely.

And there is plenty of pushback from other generators who simply don't want a transmission provider to encroach upon their territory, which brings us to the second problem for Oncor: it is facing opposition from within its own corporate family. Energy Future Holdings (EFH), Oncor's parent company, also owns power generator Luminant and retail provider TXU Energy, who released a joint statement claiming that Oncor's plan would unfairly undermine the competitive nature of the retail sector.

As this is precisely what deregulation sought to enhance, it may seem that Oncor's efforts to get the law's language changed to allow it to install the batteries and monetize them will face fierce opposition. However, Oncor does have support from State Senator Troy Fraser, who has said that he is enthusiastic about the increase in reliability and capacity that the batteries would provide, but that he is searching for a "revenue-neutral" option that wouldn't unfairly shift costs from one energy sector to another. As Fraser is the Chairman of the Texas Senate Committee on Natural Resources, he is a strong ally to have.

Battery storage has long been thought of as a way to help better integrate renewable energy sources such as wind and solar into the electricity grid. Texas is the largest producer of wind power in the US. The big problem with wind, however, is intermittency. At times (often at night) wind can produce more power that the grid requires. At other times, when the wind isn’t blowing, the grid could run short on power. This is a big shortcoming that fossil fuel sources like coal and natural gas don’t have. Large scale battery storage could solve this problem. But, until recently, battery storage technology was too inefficient and expensive to be practical.  

 

Holbert Janson is a father, business owner, and editor of the Texas Chamber of Commerce Energy Association newsletter. He is passionate about energy and the state of Texas.
