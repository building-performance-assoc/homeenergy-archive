Can economic growth truly become green?

By Cate Palmer · 16.08.2017

Economic growth usually comes at the expense of the environment and that’s what humanity has been trying to fight for – for the last few decades at least. The mainstream environmental narrative suggests that not a lot of things are about to change for the better. We’re still struggling to become more environmentally friendly and less destructive. However, there is a bright point and that’s economic growth that has been oriented towards preserving the environment. The experiences of China and India already point to a different paradigm: once countries begin to modernize, there is a turning point at which citizens come to demand cleaner air and water and other environmental protections. Better tools for measuring investments in “green growth” can accelerate this transition.

The same goes for poor countries. For example, solar panels are the future of our energy. Very few can afford it, though, as it is a long-term investment. Can poor countries also become green? To them, the obsession of some rich people with preserving forests and saving animals, while paying less and less attention to the human beings living nearby, is both cynical and hypocritical. If we’re saving our planet, aren’t we doing it for the benefit of the humanity as well? And, basically, man is what happened to Earth in the first place when it comes to pollution and extinction.

There is, of course, plenty of evidence that greenery and growth are not polar opposites. After decades of expansion in China and other fast-emerging economies, some of the negative side effects and their impact on human welfare, above all the death toll caused by polluted air and water, are horribly clear. China has experienced spectacular growth in the past 35 years with a better living standard for all classes; and while the growing middle class in China now can buy cars, go shopping, and enjoy a richer diet, they suffer from extreme smog in cities and water pollution. However – they’re at a turning point.

Increasing prosperity in the country has also enabled environmental concerns, which then become a potent political force to which the central government must respond to. In 2014, the first environmental protection law was enacted. In doing so for the first time in history, the Chinese government is in the process of changing the cadre evaluation system to include the environment.

Solar energy and self-driving vehicles

Sounds almost like something out of a sci-fi movie, right? Well, according to the director of the Global Green Growth Institute (GGGI), it’s evident that some major changes in our economy will occur. Solar energy and self-driving vehicles will probably take over, just like cars took over carriages a century ago. The future will allow us to call a self-driving car with the push of a button – ending the need for private car ownership, which basically means less air pollution. Those cars will, naturally, be electric.

The demand growth for oil and coal will be put to a stop if this revolution in energy sector lets solar panels and electric vehicles dominate in the near future. He also mentioned that significant volumes of investment are shifting away from fossil fuels and towards alternative energy services, particularly in countries with binding renewable energy targets such as in Europe, so this really seems like a possible reality to us.

Green companies

As global warming becomes an increasingly prevalent topic amongst world governments, many companies are doing their part to become environmentally more responsible, or "green". The community and social risk of climate change challenges are serious business risks – businesses simply don’t flourish in failing societies and it’s difficult to separate community well-being from companies’ viability and, in turn, economic growth. Business response to climate change forms part of the transition to a green economy, defined as one that is low-carbon, resource-efficient and socially inclusive.

It goes on to say that, in a green economy, growth in income and employment can be generated by strategic public and private investments that reduce GHG (greenhouse gas) emissions, improve resource efficiency and reduce loss of biodiversity. Some of the examples can be found anywhere in the world as more and more companies are starting to feel safer and more efficient by becoming green – something as simple as construction rubbish collection can be turned into a fantastic message to the world, as they not only remove debris and garbage, they also separate it and recycle for their customers who don’t have the time to do that themselves.

Greening the growth path of an economy depends on policy and institutional settings, level of development, social structures, resources, funding and specific environmental pressure points. Advanced, emerging, and developing countries will face different challenges. While national plans will differ, in all cases green growth strategies need to go hand-in-hand with the main pillars of action to promote social equity: more intensive human capital investment, inclusive employment promotion, and well-designed tax/transfer redistribution policies.
