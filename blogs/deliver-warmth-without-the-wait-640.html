Deliver Warmth Without the Wait

By Jonah Schein · 08.01.2015

Despite today’s mobile technology, same-day delivery, and live streaming conveniences, there is one thing people still wait for at home: hot water. Just as ill-planned ductwork can decrease a home’s energy performance (even with efficient equipment and insulation), a poorly designed plumbing system can make homeowners wait for—and waste—hot water.

Hot water that needs to travel through pipes with circuitous routes, a larger diameter than is necessary, or excessively long runs wastes both energy and water. By working with your plumbing trade partners to design a delivery system that maximizes efficiency and increases convenience, you can help reduce a home’s utility costs and add value to homeowners and prospective home buyers.

Efficient Plumbing Design: An Untapped Market

Many raters and building science professionals are in the business of helping homeowners save energy, but reducing water use still represents an untapped market to improve both the efficiency and performance of homes. It takes a significant amount of energy to heat, treat, and deliver water, making water efficiency one of the best new tools for energy-conscious builders and home buyers. Homes with electric water heaters, for example, spend one-fourth of their total electric bills just to heat water. Meanwhile, news of unprecedented drought and failing infrastructure are making water efficiency one of the hot buttons for today’s resource-conscious consumers. And while building science technology has helped residential energy use for heating and cooling decrease by 25 percent over the past few decades, the energy used by homes to heat water has actually increased, despite substantial gains in the efficiencies of individual products (e.g., showerheads, clothes washers). This is in part because waiting for hot water to reach the tap wastes the average household more than an estimated 3,650 gallons of water per year.

Hot water distribution systems (i.e., the portion of a home’s plumbing system that moves hot water from the heater to the point of use) are often given short shrift when constructing a new home or addition. Bids are primarily evaluated based on cost and schedule, and plumbers are expected to install pipes as quickly and inexpensively as possible. How well the system performs isn’t always addressed, but considering it will take a day to install, be used for 50 years or more, and serve as one of the home’s most intensive users of energy and water, hot water delivery deserves more attention to detail.

Thoughtful design can provide efficient distribution systems without causing major increases in cost or installation time. As a home energy professional, you can remind builders, contractors, and homeowners of the benefits of good plumbing design, just as you would for air distribution. Installing a high-performing plumbing system ultimately saves homeowners money and allows contractors to deliver a better performing home with fewer call-backs.

Branch Out by Using WaterSense

Hot water delivery plays such a big role in household water and energy consumption that the WaterSense new home specification requires that plumbing systems meet specific performance measures. To minimize water loss, the plumbing system in a WaterSense labeled home can store no more than 0.5 gallons of water between the hot water source (typically the water heater, but can also be a recirculating line in some cases) and the furthest fixture in the home. This feature alone can save homeowners enough electricity to power their television for an entire year. In addition, any recirculation system must be demand-initiated, drawing hot water into the recirculation loop only when needed.

Following is a brief summary of plumbing delivery systems as a starting point for discussing hot water delivery with new construction and remodeling contractors; more information can be found in the WaterSense Guide for Efficient Hot Water Delivery Systems. Although individual designs vary by project, there are four basic hot water delivery system types that are used in single-family homes:

  • Trunk and branch systems: The most common type of hot water delivery system, this design also has tremendous potential to be inefficient if care isn't taken to centralize fixture placement and minimize pipe run lengths. Trunk and branch systems are most suitable for smaller homes or homes with fewer fixtures grouped tightly together.
  • Core systems: Core systems can be more efficient because they utilize a central plumbing core (preferably with the heater located in the center) and place plumbing-heavy areas such as kitchens, bathrooms, and laundries in close proximity to one another and to the water heater. Smaller diameter piping is used where appropriate in the most direct route possible, which reduces the volume of water stored between the heater and any one fixture—and thus, wait time for hot water to arrive at fixtures.
  • Whole-house manifold systems: Also known as parallel pipe or home run systems, this design utilizes small-diameter, flexible pipes (typically PEX) that run directly from a central manifold to each fixture. The flexible piping means fewer fittings are required during installation and allows for redirection in continuous turns, eliminating the need for elbow fittings that can result in heat loss. Whole-house manifold systems are ideal for larger homes with spacious layouts and multiple fixtures.
  • Demand-initiated recirculation systems: You probably already know how demand-initiated systems that recirculate the water throughout the home are more energy-efficient than always-on recirculation systems, because hot water is only drawn into the recirculation loop when needed. They also save water by reducing the wait time for hot water to nearly nothing, and by returning ambient-temperature water stored in the piping back to the heater.

Putting an end to the hot water waiting game through careful plumbing design benefits both customers and contractors. Learn more about how WaterSense is helping homes save water and energy at www.epa.gov/watersense.

 

Jonah Schein is the Technical Coordinator for Homes and Buildings at WaterSense, a partnership program sponsored by EPA that seeks to protect the future of our nation’s water supply by offering people a simple way to use less water with water-efficient products, new homes, and services. Since the program’s inception in 2006, WaterSense has helped consumers save 757 billion gallons of water and $14.2 billion in water and energy bills.
