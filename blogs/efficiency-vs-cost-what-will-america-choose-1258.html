Efficiency vs. Cost: What Will America Choose?

By Priyanka Khan · 29.01.2020

In the wake of 2020, America sheds controversial light on its Energy-efficiency standards.  

 

On January 15, 2020, the National Electrical Manufacturers Association (NEMA) and American Lighting Association (ALA) withdrew a Federal Preemption lawsuit that they filed against California earlier.

 

The lawsuit filed on December 13, 2019, demanded a temporary restraining order on the new energy standards set by California on General Service Lamps. The basis of filing this lawsuit was that there could be only one definition of the GSL in the ‘Energy Policy and Conservation Act, 2007’ enacted by Congress.

 

While commenting on California’s decision to enforce the energy-efficiency standards, an anonymous source from ALA thinks that it will set a dangerous precedent. This source further mentioned that ALA has been fighting because the Department of Energy established a preemption over the GSL product category, and “California is basically going out and setting its own definition for the General Service Lamps under authority it doesn’t have.”

 

This source also sardonically stated California’s new enactment as a “Quasi-Definition” that will cause compliant issues and limit consumer choice in the state of California.

 

Concerned by the new energy standard set by California, NEMA and ALA filed the case in the hope of getting a year of injunction or a temporary restraining order. The appeal lacked merit as the Federal District Court denied their motion. “Plaintiff could not demonstrate the likelihood of irreparable harm in the absence of preliminary relief,” the court order read. 

 

In a press release issued regarding the withdrawal of the case Clark Silcox, General Counsel (NEMA) said, “The court’s decision on the temporary restraining order implies, without deciding, that there might be two definitions: one for California only and one for the rest of the country. NEMA believes that this contradicts Congress’s clear statement that federal energy conservation regulation is a comprehensive national policy”. 

 

Even after dismissing the Californian enactment and further taking it to court, NEMA and ALA withdrew their complaint, which raises some questions whether they had merit behind their lawsuit. 

The Californian point of view

Contrary to ALA’s concern (that California has broken the law by “ignoring the federal preemption”), California Energy Commission’s spokesperson Amber Pasricha Beck highlights significant facts.

 

Amber says, “California and Nevada were given the right to exempt from preemption by Congress, that allowed the state to adopt the 45 lumens per watt standard before the rest of the nation.” 

 

“Congress gave authority to the state of California to adopt early the Energy Independence and Security Act of 2007. The act laid a minimum efficacy standard of 45 lumens per watt on General Service Lamps, which would have become effective at the beginning of January 1, 2020, nationwide,” she continues. 

 

Therefore, the state enjoys an exemption from following the DOE’s standards for energy saving and did not set any dangerous precedent, says Beck. On sharing her opinion on the lawsuit, she says that the case was about a different definition set by them. And how “two Industry Associations” (NEMA and ALA) were raising questions on their authority to set this energy standard. She affirms that California’s right to this effective energy-saving enactment as not an outside law from the Federal preemption.

The story so far

The Energy Policy and Conservation Act of 1975, directed that DOE is to initiate a rulemaking for GSL that would determine its standards for amends. For the first rulemaking cycle, Congress instructed DOE to initiate a rulemaking process before January 1, 2014. In this, the DOE must consider whether to amend the standards of GSL or irradiate with them. 

 

Further, if the Secretary determines that there should be amends in the standards, in effect for GSL, EPCA provides a final rule that must be published by January 1, 2017. The compliance date set for this standard to come in effect is at least three years after the date of its final publication. 

 

In this development, DOE must consider a minimum efficacy standard of 45 lumens per watt. If DOE fails to complete this rulemaking, the statute will provide a “backstop” under which DOE must prohibit the sale of GSLs. 

 

The story took a new turn on February 11, 2019, when the DOE published a notice of proposed rulemaking (NOPR) proposing to withdraw the revised definitions of GSL. The revised GSL definition and other supplementary definitions were to go on effect on January 1, 2020. The DOE proposed the final ruling on September 5, 2019, to withdraw the final definition established on January 19, 2019. 

 

Eventually, on December 20, 2019, DOE submitted its final determination that they would not accept the amended definition stating that “the benefits of more stringent standards do not outweigh the cost to the American people.”  

America! Wake up to a brighter light

 

Without getting caught up in the war between Federal and State statutory laws, we as Americans should focus on the main motif of the efficient energy-saving standard. 

 

It is the collaborative efforts of the government and the community that helps bring positive reforms. Think about the catastrophe Americans would have faced if the government had not enforced motorbike helmets in 1967, saying these are “more stringent standards.”  

 

The fate of LEDs hangs in a legal battle in the US Second Circuit Court. At the same time, global warming is becoming a greater threat to the environment each passing day. We can not reverse the damage; nonetheless, making smart decisions is the need of time. Making the shift to energy-efficient led lights, while there is still time, will help keep a check on carbon footprint. 

 

It’s high time that America must decide which way to light!
