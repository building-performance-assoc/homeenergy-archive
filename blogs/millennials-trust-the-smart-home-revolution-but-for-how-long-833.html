Millennials Trust the Smart Home Revolution, but For How Long?

By Andy Heikkila · 26.04.2016

Brands and products that aren’t trusted by Millennials are doomed to fail, with Gen Y making up more and more of the general market-base than ever before. Fortunately for the green and the smart home movement, Millennials currently do trust and want more smart home technology in their lives—but for how long? With looming threats on cybersecurity on the horizon, Millennial trust in the smart home movement may just have been set up to get knocked right back down again.

Millennials Are Adopting Smart Homes...

A report from Connected Intelligence claims that Millennials are twice as likely as the total population to have a smart home product installed in their residence, and that 23% of all Millennials already live with a smart home product, as compared to 12% of the average market. This report also posits that 41% of Millennials are aware of and interested in owning smart home products, a feat made possible by the fact that renters--many of whom are Millennials themselves--are able to join the revolution due to the portable nature of many smart home products.

Currently, Phoenix, Dallas, and New York (in that order) are the three cities leading the market in smart-home adoption. Surprisingly, Silicon Valley, generally considered the breeding ground for American innovation, is not leading the charge in adoption. In fact, they are not even considered one of the top 10 smart home markets in the U.S. right now. Phoenix attributes the smart home explosion to the number of single family homes being built by large scale builders in the area, mirroring Harris Poll results that show 78% of Americans expect newly built homes to include smart home technology within the next five years.

...Or Are They?

Interestingly, an Earnest poll tracking students and Millennial living and moving habits found that a quarter of all people who graduated from a top 25 school outside the West Coast moved there within five years of graduation. Combined with the stat that 88% of West Coast graduates stay on the west, the poll seems to contradict the idea that Millennials are actually adopting smart home practices, lending more credibility to the idea that they want smart home gadgets and devices, but simply can’t afford them.

Nevertheless, the home automation movement will keep on gaining traction, and Millennials will continue to sit at the center of the industry’s attention long after the technology tips into the general market. The reason? Millennials don’t trust anybody—but does the smart home movement have anything to hide?

Millennial Trust

It’s hard to overstate how crucial Millennial trust is to any brand, with the Washington Post claiming that said trust is driving the new economy. It’s also hard to overstate how thin Millennial trust is and how jaded many of them are already, especially in the face of new environmentalism. On one hand you have this new public interest in the green movement where consumers are finally realizing that we only have one world to live in and that the fate of it is largely in corporate hands. On the other, you have major institutions concerned more with “profit-at-all-costs” than they are with consumer interests, let alone the environmental fate of the planet. A quick look at the headlines in last three years is quite revealing: EPA-related fracking controversies, the California methane leak, the VW diesel scandal, and Exxon’s lies concerning the risks of climate change are just a few of the major events that have taken center stage in our enlightened era.

Of course, at this point, smart home manufacturers are asking what type of Millennial trust they could possibly breach? To understand the lesser known threats inherent in smart homes, first one must understand what the Internet of Things (IoT) is and how it relates to home automation.

The IoT and the Looming Threat of Ransomware

The IoT refers to all of the devices--your iPhone, your Nest device, even your office refrigerator nowadays—that are connected to the internet. These devices have sensors in them that track the temperature of your house, for example, and relay that data over the Internet to your phone so that you can turn on your A/C before you get home on a hot summer day, or turn off the heat as you leave for winter vacation.

The amazing abilities of smart homes are driven by their connectivity to the IoT. Unfortunately, automation’s biggest strength may also be its biggest weakness. It’s been known since at least mid-2014 that the IoT is wildly insecure, and even bigger alarm bells began to sound in 2015 when experts realized that the IoT is setting the perfect stage for rampant ransomware outbreaks that are already holding our homes, cars, and hospitals hostage. Ransomware refers to a specific type of malware that shuts down a system or causes an object to malfunction. In order to return the device to normal functionality, the user is prompted to pay the attacker’s “ransom”, hence the name ransomware.

Fortunately, none of the cases have been too serious and no deaths have occurred as a result of these types of attacks, although one L.A. hospital was forced to pay a $17,000 ransom to get their own networks back online. While no deaths have occurred as a result of a ransomware attack, and no major consumer breaches have made headlines, there are still those that are expressing concern: it’s only a matter of time. What would you do if your A/C was cranked to an unbearable 45 degrees and the only way to stop the arctic blast was to pay a ransom?

Fortunately, it’s not necessarily going to take a “Cyber 9/11” for the industry to realize that serious upgrades in cyber security are imperative for the smart home market; the minute that any serious smart home breach makes headlines, Millennials are going to start looking at smart home providers who can promise reliable protection.
