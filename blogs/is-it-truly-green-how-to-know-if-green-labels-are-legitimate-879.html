Is it truly Green? How to Know if Green Labels Are Legitimate

By Chloe Taylor · 20.09.2016

As environmentalists continue to warn against growing greenhouse gas emission and dwindling natural resources, eco-friendly labels are getting more popular by the day in all segments of life, including energy, building, and interior design. Still, is every product that has a green stamp truly worth the name? Unfortunately, it’s not: greenwashing is a common advertising scheme that’s ruining both the industry and consumers’ trust in eco-marketing campaigns. But is it possible for an average consumer to tell apart a real green deal from a smart sales slogan, and how can it be done?

1. Green As Certified

One of the easiest ways to spot a greenwashed product from a distance is the type of certificate displayed on its packaging. To earn an eco-friendly certificate, a brand has to provide all relevant data to the third-party certification authority to substantiate the claims about a product’s environmental safety and to ensure maximum transparency in the process. Brands that have a legitimate green certificate to back the eco-safe nature of their merchandise often publicly feature on-site verification to prove the goods is compliant with all relevant standards, such as LEED, Energy Star, Green Seal, Green Guard, Green Label, Green-e, or Cradle to Cradle.

2. Do Your Homework

Greenwashing has been around for years, and it is only of late that the authorities began to pay closer attention to eco-advertizing and the true nature of products marketed as eco-friendly. In 2013, the Federal Trade Commission stepped up the efforts to identify and penalize misleading advertising campaigns. Still, if you don’t want to bank on brands’ honesty just yet you should do your homework before making the purchase and stick to trustworthy appliance stores that take green claims seriously. Instead of trusting packaging labels, go online and research companies’ websites: brands that have the goods to support the eco-friendly declarations are those that are not averse to disclosing online all the relevant info about materials, production procedures, and certificates behind their offering.

 

3. Detailed Shade of Green

Another common indicator of greenwashing, labels that assert the product as green without any other details to back the claim should be enough of a red flag for an average consumer. General statements such as ‘Eco-friendly’ or ‘Non-toxic’ you’ll come across on product packaging need to be further reinforced by specific details such as product features, benefits, materials, and energy ratings. If all you’re seeing on the package is just a short phrase and no particulars, it’s as loud a warning as it can get for you to think twice before showing the cashier your money and taking the product home. An eco-label should be accompanied by a reliable certificate (c.f. No. 1), which should also be displayed elsewhere on the product documents such as manufacturer’s warranty and instruction manual.

4. Too Good to Be Green

However eye-catching, packaging is another tool brands use to sway consumers into purchasing their not-so-green merchandise. The fact that a vacuum cleaner comes in a package made from 100% recycled cardboard doesn’t make the unit any greener: it just means you should double-check energy ratings and list of materials used in production. If the list of chemicals and paints used in the manufacturing is a mile long, odds are the product you’re holding isn’t green in the least – and if you’re uncertain about specific terms you come across on the label, all the info you need to make the final decision is just a quick online query away.

5. Down to Green Business

If you’re new to energy efficiency and all the green language brands slap on the packaging, you may want to educate yourself on the subject before you go shopping. For instance, a common system used to distinguish green from non-eco appliances is the efficiency scale ranging from D to A+++, where D stands for least energy-smart and A+++ indicates top-rate energy efficiency. Retailers still sell washing machines, dishwashers, and refrigerators with ratings below A, but now that you know what the symbols means, you’ll be able to pick the right product for your green home and save cash through higher long-term electricity savings.

It may take a good while for all the brands to stop greenwashing merchandise for higher profits and finally truly go green with their products, but the recent crackdown on corporations that take eco-labels lightly are a ray of hope. Still, don’t board the eco-friendly wagon unless you’re absolutely certain that the goods you’re paying in your hard-earned greens are actually as green as they’re advertized to be – and if you follow the steps listed above, your household will always be on the environmentally safe side of the equation.
