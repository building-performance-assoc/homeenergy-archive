Trends and Opportunities in Home Energy End Uses

By Charlie Hewitt · 20.01.2015

Gone are the days when a programmable thermostat and dimmable lights equated to sound home energy management. The latest generation of technology includes smart thermostats, Wi-Fi enabled lighting, and home automation systems. Some of these technologies reduce energy consumption while others are just plain gimmicky. Optimizing space heating and having a refrigerator that can play music while accessing a cooking website are not in the same category. They both have value but we need to separate energy management from simply trying to emulate the Jetsons.

Taking a closer look at how far we have come in reducing home energy intensities and the end uses driving those changes is a good starting point.

Efficiency Improvements

Homes are far more energy efficient than they used to be. U.S. Department of Energy (USDOE) analysis shows that homes built between 2000 and 2005 use 14% less energy per square foot than homes built in the 1980s. Even more remarkably, they use 40% less energy than homes built before 1950. The homes used in the 2000 to 2005 sample group were not equipped with smart thermostats, advanced lighting controls, or musical refrigerators. Energy efficiency gains from a smart energy home built in 2015 would be significantly higher.

Average annual household energy usage in 1980 was 197.4 MMBtu. In 2010, it dropped to only 193.3 MMBtu. With all of the efficiency gains during this period, why did the total energy usage drop by only 2%? The answer lies with the size of our homes. In 1950, the average single-family house was a cozy 983 square feet. By 1980 this increased to a roomier 1740 square feet and then to 2392 square feet in 2010.

The good news is that the growth in home size is flattening out while energy efficiency is increasing every year. The USDOE reflects this in their projection of average household energy usage dropping to 171.9 MMBtu by the year 2020.

Energy End Use Trends

The USDOE projects that the top five residential energy end uses in 2015 will be space heating, space cooling, water heating, lighting, and refrigeration. This is not surprising but it is noteworthy that these end uses constitute just over 70% of total household usage. This percentage drops by about 2% per decade through 2035.

What are the trends within these projections? One important element is people migrating to warmer regions. Space heating usage decreases while space cooling usage increases. Market penetration of smart thermostats will also increase. Utilities and retail electric providers will continue to develop rate structures that incentivize the use of smart meters. It may take two decades but space conditioning energy usage will decrease.

Lighting usage decreases from 7.6 to 5.9% of total household usage between 2015 and 2035. This is largely the result of improvements in lighting technologies including bulbs, motion sensors, and smart home programming.

Usage gains will come from electronics and other small electrics. While electronics have consolidated (remember the stereo, VCR, and DVD player?), the number of devices in each home and number of hours of use per day are increasing.

Refrigeration, laundry and dishwashing, or cooking energy usage remains flat from 2015 to 2035 as technological advances and smart home improvements will not significantly affect these end uses.

Water Heating: An Opportunity for Improvement

Homeowners are installing smart thermostats, revamping light fixtures with compact fluorescents, and programming their lights with their phones. The last big target is water heating. Water heating uses a whopping 10 to 14% of total household energy. Standard tank water heaters have limited room for efficiency improvements. Measures like insulating the water heater and hot water pipes or lowering the temperature will only get you so far. Homeowners can reduce hot water consumption by using a high efficiency washing machine. In addition, innovative showerheads are available that create larger water droplets giving the feeling of standard flow while actually using less water.

The future in water heating lies with demand or instantaneous water heaters. These tankless systems heat water only when there is a demand for hot water. Standby losses from the tank are eliminated which reduces wasted energy. However, retrofitting to a tankless water heater can be cost prohibitive. Widely separated hot water uses in the home can also reduce the efficiency of the system. These challenges need to be resolved in the context of overall household energy management. Without integration, water heating efficiency improvements will be limited.
